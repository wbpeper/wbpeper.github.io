Four-Bar LED Positioning Linkage

A single-motor four-bar mechanism designed, simulated, and controlled to position an LED at four target locations under geometric, mechanical, electrical, and packaging constraints.

Read the full final reportBack to projects

01 · Problem & requirements

From four target positions to one controlled mechanism

The objective was to develop a single-motor linkage capable of positioning an LED at four required target locations. The mechanism had to fit the available package, remain manufacturable, move with low inertia, and achieve repeatable positioning while keeping total predicted power below 1 W.

02 · Linkage fundamentals

Optimizing force transmission—not just reaching the targets

Transmission angle describes how effectively force is transferred between links in a four-bar mechanism. Configurations near unfavorable transmission angles can reduce mechanical advantage and make it harder for the motor to drive the mechanism reliably.

The design therefore did more than remain inside the allowable range of 30° ≤ μ ≤ 150°. It sought to minimize the maximum deviation from 90° so force transmission remained more favorable across the operating range.

Final maximum transmission-angle deviation: approximately 33.6°Geometry was evaluated against both workspace requirements and force-transmission quality.

Moving mass was a competing objective. Reducing mass lowers rotational inertia, which reduces the torque required for acceleration and supports lower power consumption. Mass minimization alone was not sufficient: the linkage still needed acceptable transmission angles, stiffness, workspace, manufacturability, and reliable physical operation.

04 · Dynamic simulation

MSC ADAMS informed design selection

MSC ADAMS moved the evaluation beyond static CAD geometry. Candidate linkage designs were compared using their actual motion, input behavior, torque requirements, dynamic response, power consumption, and the effects of geometry and inertia.

This made simulation part of design selection rather than merely a final validation step. The piecewise input motion also matters when interpreting the plots: changes in the commanded input can produce discontinuities in plotted torque, so the result must be read in the context of the prescribed motion rather than treated as unexplained physical behavior.

Final simulated maximum power: approximately 0.92 WThis remained below the 1 W system requirement and connected the geometry/inertia decisions to a measurable electrical constraint.

05 · Selection & torque transfer

Balancing geometry, inertia, and mechanical integrity

Final selection considered transmission angles, moving mass, motor/load inertia, workspace, and manufacturability together. Inertia matching was used in selecting the drivetrain transmission, with a final 1.6 gear transmission ratio where supported by the report.

The final torque-transfer analysis checked the mechanical interfaces rather than relying only on nominal component availability. The input-link gear joint carried approximately 11.9 lb·in of torque, and the steel spring pin was checked in shear to verify that the selected interface could safely transmit the expected load.

06 · Controls integration

Closing the loop around the mechanism

An Arduino-based controller used Hall encoder feedback, PWM motor control, a proximity sensor, and homing behavior through a limit switch. The controller compared desired and actual position and adjusted the motor rather than relying on open-loop timing.

PID feedback supported repeatable movement between the four target locations. Position and velocity information allowed the system to correct tracking error, while friction compensation was applied where applicable. The controls work was therefore directly tied to the mechanical requirement: a lightweight linkage still needed reliable physical positioning.
