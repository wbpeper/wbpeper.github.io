Four-Bar LED Positioning Linkage

A single-motor four-bar mechanism designed, simulated, built, and controlled to position an LED at four required target locations.

01 · Problem & requirements

From four target positions to one controlled mechanism

The objective was to develop a single-motor linkage capable of positioning an LED at four required target locations. The mechanism had to fit the available package, remain manufacturable, move smoothly without jamming, and operate with power consumption below 1 W.

RequirementDesign implication
Four target positionsLinkage workspace had to cover each required location.
Single motor (≤1 W)Efficient geometry and low inertia were critical.
Small footprintCompact link lengths and careful joint placement.
ManufacturabilityParts suitable for laser cutting and simple assembly.
Smooth motionNo binding, jamming, or dead centers in the workspace.

02 · Linkage fundamentals

Optimizing force transmission—not just reaching the targets

Transmission angle describes how effectively force is transferred between links in a four-bar mechanism. Configurations near unfavorable transmission angles can reduce mechanical advantage and increase torque demand. A commonly cited acceptable range is 30° ≤ μ ≤ 150°.

The design therefore did more than remain inside the allowable range of 30° ≤ μ ≤ 150°. It sought to minimize the maximum deviation from 90° so force transmission remained close to optimal across the entire working range.

Final maximum transmission-angle deviation: approximately 33.6°Geometry was evaluated against both workspace requirements and force-transmission efficiency. No point in the mechanism's motion exceeded 150° or fell below 30°.

Moving mass was a competing objective. Reducing mass lowers rotational inertia, which reduces the torque required for acceleration and supports lower power consumption. Mass minimization alone could compromise geometry or manufacturability, so the final design balanced all constraints.

03 · CAD & kinematics

Using geometry to prove the mechanism could reach every position

CAD configurations were used to evaluate the mechanism across its required positions. The four-bar layout demonstrated geometric feasibility and showed the coupler/LED path through the target workspace.

04 · Dynamic simulation

MSC ADAMS informed design selection

MSC ADAMS moved the evaluation beyond static CAD geometry. Candidate linkage designs were compared using their actual motion, input behavior, torque requirements, dynamic response, power consumption, and repeatability under load.

This made simulation part of design selection rather than merely a final validation step. The piecewise input motion also matters when interpreting the plots: changes in the commanded input can appear as discontinuities in torque and power, but they represent intentional control changes rather than mechanical failures.

Final simulated maximum power: approximately 0.92 WThis remained below the 1 W system requirement and connected the geometry/inertia decisions to actual performance under the commanded motion profile.

05 · Selection & torque transfer

Balancing geometry, inertia, and mechanical integrity

Final selection considered transmission angles, moving mass, motor/load inertia, workspace, and manufacturability together. Inertia matching was used in selecting the drivetrain transmission, which included a gearbox to reduce motor speed and increase torque availability.

The final torque-transfer analysis checked the mechanical interfaces rather than relying only on nominal component availability. The input-link gear joint carried approximately 11.9 lb-in under peak dynamic load, well within the rated capacity of the selected components.

06 · Controls integration

Closing the loop around the mechanism

An Arduino-based controller used Hall encoder feedback, PWM motor control, a proximity sensor, and homing behavior through a limit switch. The controller compared desired and actual position and adjusted motor voltage in real time.

PID feedback supported repeatable movement between the four target locations. Position and velocity information allowed the system to correct tracking error, while friction compensation was applied during low-speed transitions. The final system reliably reached all four target positions with minimal overshoot.
