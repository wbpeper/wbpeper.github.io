Robotic Mobility Platform

A mechanically designed and manufactured vehicle integrating a planetary-gearbox drivetrain, motorized scoop, collection system, structure, and electromechanical controls.

01 · Requirements

From physical requirements to vehicle architecture

The project required a mobile robotic platform capable of collecting material with a powered scoop. The final architecture combined a planetary-gearbox-powered drivetrain, a motorized collection mechanism, a structural frame, and integrated electrical controls.

The central mechanical challenge was converting vehicle behavior into defensible component requirements before selecting motors or gearboxes. The design process followed:

Required behavior→Forces & resistance→Wheel torque→Reduction→Drivetrain

02 · Drivetrain analysis

First-principles sizing of the drive system

Rather than choosing a drivetrain through trial and error, the analysis started with the forces the vehicle needed to overcome. The required wheel torque was derived from the expected resistance and wheel geometry, then compared with available motor torque to determine the reduction required.

Wheel torque requirementτwheel = Fresistance · rwheelResistance and wheel radius establish the torque required at the ground interface.
Reduction requirementGR ≥ (τwheel · FS) / τmotorThe selected ratio includes the 1.75 factor of safety to provide margin for friction, tolerances, loading, and real-world resistance.
Calculated drivetrain requirement: approximately 344:1The analysis drove the selection of a high-reduction planetary-gearbox drivetrain rather than treating gearing as an afterthought.

03 · Scoop analysis

Translating scoop load into gearing

The scoop was analyzed separately because its load acts through a moment arm. The expected load and scoop geometry were used to calculate the actuation torque, which was then compared with motor capability and increased with the design margin.

Scoop actuation logicτscoop = Fload · rmoment armRequired torque was translated into motor reduction using the available motor torque and the same safety-minded design approach.
Calculated scoop gearing requirement: approximately 100:1The ratio connected the expected collection load directly to the final motorized scoop design.

04 · CAD, manufacturing & assembly

Design decisions changed during the physical build

CAD was used to package the drivetrain, motor placement, structural members, and collection mechanism as one system. The mechanical layout had to preserve access for assembly while coordinating interfaces between mechanical components and the electrical system.

Scoop attachment: designing for assembly

The initial scoop attachment used two circular mounting holes on each side. During assembly, independently manufactured components had to align at all four mounting points simultaneously, making small positional deviations disproportionately difficult to manage.

The arms and scoop were revised to use slots rather than only fixed circular holes. The slots added controlled adjustment during installation, allowing the scoop to be aligned despite normal manufacturing and assembly variation. This was a practical design-for-assembly improvement discovered through the physical build.

The scoop was 3D printed, which simplified fabrication of its geometry compared with producing the same shape through conventional machining or fabrication. The manufacturing method supported rapid iteration while the attachment redesign addressed tolerance and alignment directly.

05 · Prototype & final system

Connecting analysis to the completed vehicle

Physical testing of the basket and collection system informed decisions about the scoop geometry and material. The completed platform brought together the calculated drivetrain and scoop requirements, structural design, collection system, and electromechanical integration.
